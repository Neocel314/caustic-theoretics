There was a time when going online felt like leaving somewhere. You sat down at a particular machine, listened to it negotiate a connection, and entered a space that was recognisably elsewhere. The distinction was imperfect, but it existed. You could disconnect.

Now the network has no entrance. It is the medium in which the ordinary takes place: the train ticket, the conversation, the photograph you take before you have quite finished looking. The outside has not been prohibited. It has become difficult to locate.

This is not an argument for returning to a more innocent internet. That internet was never as innocent as we remember. It is an attempt to name a change in the texture of experience: the gradual loss of a place from which to look back.

## Everything is already inside

The most effective systems do not silence their critics. They give them an account, a dashboard, and a way to measure their reach. Opposition acquires a format. A refusal becomes a recognisable position, then an audience, then a market.

There is something exhausting about watching this happen to your own thoughts. You begin with an irritation that feels specific and unprocessed. Before you have understood it, you encounter the vocabulary in which it is expected to appear. The thought finds its category before it finds its shape.

The problem is not that expression has become impossible. There is more expression than ever. The problem is that expression and circulation have become so tightly bound that a thought which does not circulate begins to seem like a thought which does not exist.

> A space for thought may be a space in which nothing needs to happen to the thought immediately.

## The small refusal

Perhaps the outside is no longer a place. Perhaps it is a practice of interrupting the demand to turn every experience into something legible, exchangeable, and ready to travel.

A notebook can do this. So can a conversation that nobody records. So can reading slowly enough to forget, for a while, whether the book is improving you. These are not heroic acts. Their modesty may be the point.

I am suspicious of any prescription that makes a private habit stand in for collective change. Putting down a phone does not rearrange the conditions that made it so hard to put down. But it can make those conditions visible again. A pause is not an escape. It is an opportunity to notice the walls.

The task, then, is not to discover a pure territory beyond the present. There may be no such territory. It is to preserve intervals in which the present is not mistaken for the entirety of what is possible.

To write without knowing where the writing will go. To begin a project that has no obvious use. To allow a question to remain a question for longer than feels comfortable.

Somewhere else might begin there.
